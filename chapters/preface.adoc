== Preface

This book describes Micron, a systems programming language in the school of Niklaus Wirth, and shows how to use it:
for ordinary programs, for libraries, and for an operating system that the reader can build and boot with one command.

Micron is not my first language design, and it did not appear out of nowhere.
It is the result of a long journey, and the journey is perhaps worth telling.

I studied electrical engineering at ETH Zürich from mid eighties to early nineties,
with a specialization in information technology and computer engineering.
Wirth was famous and admired among the students, and I took the chance to attend his lectures in my fifth and sixth semester,
including the compiler lectures, alongside a hundered of other students.
We were taught Modula-2 on the Macintosh, and the compilers we studied were written in Modula-2.
Oberon was developed while I was there, and Wirth might have mentioned it, but it took some more years until I encountered it.

My first close contact with Oberon came around 2000, during my doctorate,
a thesis in molecular biology and biophysics on the NMR structure determination of biological macromolecules.
An early idea was to design a programming language extending Oberon to implement the tools required by the molecular biologists,
but after initial prototypes, that endeavor turned out to be too much for the project, which even so took more than five years to complete.
I postponed the language for twenty years and implemented my scientific software in C++ with Qt instead, and with Lua as a scripting language.
Even while pursuing my doctoral studies, I took the chance to take lectures at the computer science department.
I was lucky that Bertrand Meyer arrived as Wirth's successor during those years,
and I took all his lectures, studied the world of Eiffel, and attended the guest lectures of people like Adele Goldberg,
Jean-Raymond Abrial, Dines Bjørner and Ivar Jacobson whom he invited.

The postponed idea did not go away. When I finally returned to it, I did not start with Micron.
I first built Oberon+ <<R1>>, a modernized and unified Oberon, in order to understand,
by implementing it and by porting real systems to it, what Wirth's design actually gives and where it fails.
The systems ported to Oberon+ included the 2013 Project Oberon System <<R2>> and different versions of Oberon System 3,
and I also spent significant time with the Blackbox Framework and AOS/Bluebottle including the corresponding language versions.
I then explored the opposite direction with Luon, a higher-level language for managed application programming, 
with which I implemented yet another version of the Project Oberon System, and a Smalltalk-80 virtual machine <<R3>>.
I also implemented parsers and analysis tools for Lisa Pascal, FreePascal, Simula 67, Algol 60, Mesa and Cedar, 
i.e. a whole bunch of languages along the Algol/Wirth lineage.
Micron is the systems programming conclusion of these experiments.
It is what remained when the lessons of Oberon+, the ideas that proved themselves in Luon and the other languages studied,
together with thirty years of using C, C++ and their successors, including many embedded system, 
were applied to the question that Oberon left open: 
what should a small, safe and honest language for programming machines look like?

The answer, in one sentence: Oberon's clarity and leanness with the reach of C.
The rest of this book is the long version.

=== How to read this book

The book assumes that you already program, whether in C, in Pascal or one of its descendants, in Go, in Oberon,
or in a comparable language.
It teaches Micron, not programming.

<<part1>> explains what Micron is and why it exists, and introduces the notation and the type system.
<<part2>> covers the language feature by feature, TBD.
<<part3>> walks through the Micron System, TBD.
The appendices contain everything needed to reproduce the examples, and reference material.
